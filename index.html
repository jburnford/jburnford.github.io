02 · Biography

About

I am a Professor of History at the University of Saskatchewan and Co-Editor of Historical Methods. My work is driven by a dual commitment: advancing digital methodologies and HGIS, while ensuring those tools remain firmly rooted in historical practice. I build and apply digital approaches to engage with the big questions of our time — urban environments, environmental justice, public health, global commodities, and settler colonialism.

My first book, West Ham and the River Lea (UBC Press, 2017), examined the local environmental and public health consequences of industrialization in East London. I've since followed those consequences outward — tracing tallow from London's soap factories to grasslands around the world, and timber from railway construction sites back to the forests of the Ottawa Valley. This is the research programme I call ghost acres: the distant lands that sustained industrial cities.

At the Historical GIS Lab, I build spatial databases and text-mining pipelines, map settler colonialism on the Canadian prairies, make virtual-reality experiences for public history, and help document Saskatchewan's experience of the pandemic through the Remember Rebuild community archive.

I am severely dyslexic and advocate for neurodivergent students in my teaching, research, and service work.

  4. 2026

    Reading the Archive by Machine: An OCR Benchmark for Historians, 1612–1921

    Clifford, J., Polay, J., Jack, J., Humphries, M. & Leddy, L. C.

    Working Papers in Critical Search № 004 · not peer-reviewed

    Working PaperDigital Humanities

    Abstract
    Benchmarks six OCR systems on archival documents spanning three centuries and finds that free, open-weight models a historian can run on their own hardware now match or exceed Google’s Gemini on clean print and multi-column layouts, with Gemini retaining an edge only on difficult handwriting. Argues the choice between tools has become practical rather than qualitative — turning on layout fidelity and processing speed rather than accuracy alone — and proposes a tiered workflow that pairs fast open tools with selective use of paid services while distinguishing harmless modernizations from dangerous fabrications. Read →

    A Cypher Knowledge Graph of British Imperial Territorial Evolution: Wikidata and Teamwork Without Teams in Computational History

    Clifford, J.

    Working Papers in Critical Search № 002 · not peer-reviewed

    Working PaperDigital HumanitiesSettler Colonialism

    Abstract
    A curated property graph of 747 historical British imperial territories connected by 1,203 typed relationships, every entity anchored to a Wikidata QID. Argues that Cypher is well suited to the messy genealogies of empire — partitions, reorganizations, post-imperial successor states — and that Wikidata grounding enables interoperable datasets across independent projects (“teamwork without forming teams”). Documents the human-in-the-loop verification used to keep AI coding assistants from hallucinating identifiers into the graph. Read →
